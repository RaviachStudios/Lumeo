§ 00

The bottom line

Don't fight this. Rebrand — completely, and visibly, inside the window.

On the name, Hasbro wins, and it isn't close. On the four-quadrant disc they very probably win too. Those two things go. But they are the only two things that go.

The mechanic is yours free and clear — the patent expired, the rules aren't copyrightable, and Hasbro didn't even invent it. So are the skins, the Arena, the contests, the shop, the leaderboards, the badges, the backgrounds, the music, all 500K+ lines of your code, your studio name, and your install base. You are being asked to change a title and a picture, not a product.

There is a close precedent where exactly this worked, and it took about a week. It is in §01.

§ 01

The clock, and the trap at the end of it

The single most important operational fact in this memo: you cannot fix this after suspension.

Google's message frames the deadline as "resolve the dispute within 7 days." That framing is misleading about what happens on day 8. Once Google restricts a listing for an IP complaint, the corrective update is no longer something you can simply ship — the practical route back runs through the complainant withdrawing, not through you pushing a new build.

The precedent worth reading twice

In December 2013 an indie developer, Brian DeWolff, shipped an Android game called Drisk / Droid Risk. Hasbro complained. The sequence:

  • 27 Dec — Google pulled both the paid and free versions.
  • 30 Dec — Hasbro's counsel spelled out exactly what was wrong (the name, and a store description claiming it was "the classic Risk board game") and what an acceptable fix looked like.
  • 31 Dec — Because he could no longer publish on Play, he shipped the renamed build (Border Siege) to Amazon's store to prove compliance.
  • 3 Jan — Hasbro authorised reinstatement.

Two lessons carry directly across. First, direct contact with Hasbro's counsel moved faster than anything routed through Google. Second, he had to jump through a hoop — proving compliance on a different storefront — purely because he waited until after the takedown. You still have the option he lost.

Reframe the deadline

The goal is not "make changes within 7 days." It is: get the corrected listing live and get Hasbro's counsel to say in writing that they're satisfied, before day 7. Work backwards from that. Realistically that means the letter in §07 goes out in the first 48 hours, not on day 6.

One more thing worth understanding: Play policy strikes attach to the developer account, not just the app. A single resolved complaint is a non-event. A pattern of unresolved IP complaints is what puts an account at risk. Treat this as an account-level matter and it stays a small one.

§ 02

What Hasbro actually owns

2.1  The word — and it's real

US Reg. No. 1,211,692 — SIMON. Filed 7 November 1977 by Milton Bradley Company; registered 5 October 1982; now owned by Hasbro, Inc. Status: live, registered and renewed, with ten-year renewals granted in 2002 and 2012. Typeset word mark. International Class 028, for "Equipment sold as a unit for playing an electronic type parlor game."

A lawyer looking at that registration notices two things immediately, and then has to warn you not to rely on either:

  • It is a word mark, not a design mark. On its face it covers the five letters, in any typeface. It does not, by its own terms, cover the disc.
  • It sits in Class 28 — physical game equipment. A mobile game is Class 9 / 41 goods and services.

Do not build a strategy on class

Class is a filing convenience, not the boundary of the right. Infringement turns on likelihood of confusion across related goods, and a phone app that is a faithful digital version of the exact game the registration covers is about as related as two goods get. Layer on 48 years of continuous use and near-universal public recognition and SIMON is a strong mark in its field. On the name, you lose. Plan accordingly.

2.2  The look — unregistered, but strong

The notice makes a second, separate claim: "imagery copying or mimicking the classic and iconic four-color SIMON memory game image." That is a trade dress claim, and notice what accompanies it — a link to an Amazon listing, and no registration number. It is unregistered, common-law trade dress.

That helps you less than it sounds. Under Wal-Mart Stores, Inc. v. Samara Brothers, Inc., 529 U.S. 205 (2000), product-design trade dress is never inherently distinctive — it always requires proof of secondary meaning. Hasbro clears that bar without breaking a sweat. Ask any adult in any market to draw "the electronic memory game with the coloured lights" and you will get a black circle cut into four coloured quarters.

2.3  The counter-argument, and its exact limit

There is a real functionality defence, and you should understand it — not because it wins, but because it tells you precisely how far you have to move.

  • TrafFix Devices, Inc. v. Marketing Displays, Inc., 532 U.S. 23 (2001) holds that the party asserting trade dress bears the burden of proving non-functionality, and that an expired utility patent is "strong evidence that the features therein claimed are functional." Competitors may freely copy functional features; patent law, not trademark law, sets the term of exclusivity.
  • Simon was patented: US 4,207,087, "Microcomputer controlled game," Baer & Morrison, filed 19 September 1977, granted 10 June 1980. Expired.

I read the claims — here is the catch

The patent claims the mechanic: generate a time-ordered sequence of sensory events, compare the player's responses, lengthen on success, increase speed as the sequence grows, allow the last sequence to be repeated. Where it describes hardware it specifies "four push buttons disposed in a rectangular pattern." Not a circle.

So TrafFix hands you the mechanic outright. It does not reach the disc — the circular quadrant layout was never in the claims, so the expired-patent presumption of functionality doesn't attach to it. The disc is genuinely Hasbro's strongest asset here, registration or no.

§ 03

What they don't own — and can't get

The mechanic is free

Watch a sequence of lights and tones, repeat it, it grows by one, it speeds up. Patented in 1980, expired, public domain. And Hasbro didn't originate it: Atari's arcade cabinet Touch Me shipped in 1974, four years before Simon, doing exactly this — observe a sequence of blinking lights, repeat it in order. Ralph Baer saw it and built Simon from it, adding colour and musical tones. This is why hundreds of these games exist on every app store and Hasbro sues over approximately none of them.

Game rules aren't copyrightable — but be careful how you say it

Copyright protects expression, not rules or mechanics. Do not, however, stretch this into "it's all functional, so anything goes" — that is precisely the argument that lost in Tetris Holding, LLC v. Xio Interactive, Inc., 863 F. Supp. 2d 394 (D.N.J. 2012). The court rejected the broad functionality reading and held that the visual expression of the rules — shapes, colours, playfield, look and feel — is protected. Xio had cloned the picture and lost.

The lesson is narrow and useful: copy the rules freely; do not copy the picture.

The four colours, standing alone

Red, green, blue and yellow are the obvious choice for four instantly distinguishable buttons. As part of the overall disc they are evidence; abstracted from it they aren't a mark anyone owns.

The most tempting move available to you is also the most fatal

In Simon Says Enterprises, Inc. v. Milton Bradley Co., 522 F. Supp. 986 (S.D.N.Y. 1981), a nightclub performer sued Milton Bradley over the phrase. The court had "grave doubts that the appropriation of the name of a traditional children's game by the plaintiffs amounts to a valid trademark," found no likelihood of confusion, and granted summary judgment to Milton Bradley.

That the playground game is public property does not license SIMON on an electronic memory game — which is exactly what Reg. 1,211,692 covers. Do not rename to "Simon Says," "Simon's," "SimOn," "Symon," or anything in that neighbourhood. A near-miss rename reads as bad faith and turns a routine complaint into a real one.

§ 05

How far the wheel actually has to move

Trade dress protects a combination. You don't need a different game — you need a disc that no reasonable person mistakes for Hasbro's. Break enough elements of the combination and it stops reading as theirs.

Hasbro's disc, decomposed

  1. A circular device
  2. Divided into exactly four equal quadrants
  3. Red, green, blue and yellow
  4. A heavy black bezel and a cross dividing the quarters
  5. A distinct central hub

Your default wheel currently hits all five. And critically — I checked the Wild West skin, and it hits all five too. It changes the frame colour to purple and the background to a desert; the four quadrants are the same four colours in the same arrangement. Reskinning the frame does not touch this claim. Any remediation that only swaps backgrounds will fail.

The fix is already in your codebase

game_state.gd sets num_colors = 4 / 5 / 6 for Easy / Moderate / Hard, and game.gd already declares six BUTTON_COLORS. Your own 512px app icon is already a six-segment wheel. The non-infringing wheel is built, tested, and shipping today — it just isn't the face of the app.

The specific changes, in priority order

  1. Six segments by default, not four. Simon is definitively four. This is the single highest-value change on the list and it costs you one line of configuration. Keep four-button Easy as a difficulty if you like — it just must never be the app icon, the default view, or in a screenshot.
  2. Retire the primary palette as the default. Ship a palette that's yours — cyan, violet, magenta, lime, amber, coral. You already sell colour packs; promote a non-classic set to default and let players buy their way to primaries if they want them. A palette the user chooses is a very different posture from one you ship as your brand identity.
  3. Kill the black bezel and the cross divider. Separate the pads so they read as discrete buttons on a background rather than a disc cut into pieces. The white/grey cross in your current default is a direct Simon signature.
  4. Change the hub. Your level numeral sitting in a grey circle at the centre is close to Simon's centre plate. Move the counter out of the middle, or restyle it so it doesn't read as a control panel.
  5. Apply it to every skin. Wild West, Jackpot, Arcade, Luna Park — all currently share the same four-quadrant geometry. Geometry is shared; only the dressing differs. Fix it at the geometry layer once.
§ 06

The rename, and the remediation checklist

Rules for the new name

  • Zero uses of "Simon" anywhere in the title, subtitle, short description, long description, or ASO keyword fields. In the Risk case Hasbro's counsel objected to the description, not only the title. Assume every field gets read.
  • Don't trade one live mark for another. Screen every candidate against USPTO search and a Play Store search before committing. Two attractive names illustrate the trap: Echo and Blink are both Amazon marks in Class 9. Nice names; wrong ones.
  • Descriptive names are close to unattackable — "Four Color Memory Game" is a live 100K+ install Play app that took exactly this route. The tradeoff is you can't own a descriptive name either.
  • The structure I'd choose: distinctive brand + descriptive tail. The brand carries identity and can eventually be registered as yours; the tail carries the search traffic you're losing. Format: BRAND — Memory Challenge.

Directions to screen (none of these is cleared — screen before use)

  • Playback / recall: Encore · Recall · Reprise · Mimiq · Repeat After
  • Light & tone: Lumo · Chime · Flare · Aurora · Glowline
  • Invented / studio-tied: Ravio · Sequo · Memoro · Tonari

Avoid SEQUENCE (Jax Games holds it for a game, Class 28), and anything that sounds like a Simon misspelling.

Store surfaces — Play Console

  • App title · short description · full description — every "Simon" out
  • Icon: Simon_Icon_192/432/512.png, play store/icon_*.png, new icon.png
  • Feature graphic and all screenshots — reshoot after the wheel change, not before
  • Developer name: no change. "Raviach Studios" was not challenged and shouldn't be volunteered.

User-visible strings in code

  • project.godotconfig/name="Simon" → new name
  • home_screen.gd:313The "S I M [O] N" logo where the O is a four-colour ring — rebuild the wordmark entirely. This is the most exposed single asset in the app.
  • home_screen.gd:3139, 3194, 3297, 3585, 3588, 3607Version footer, mailto subject, "Welcome to Simon", "customize Simon", "Play Simon"
  • consent_dialog.gd:17, 19"Ads in Simon" / "Simon is free to play, and ads pay for it."
  • how_to_play.gd:32"Simon lights up a sequence of colored buttons" — this one describes Hasbro's device. Change to "The wheel lights up a sequence…"
  • shop_screen.gd:49Shop tab label "SIMON" → "WHEEL" or "COLORS"
  • badge_manager.gd:127Badge "Simon Says Master" — doubly bad, it's the Simon Says form
  • contest_manager.gd:409, 413Contest names "Simon Smackdown", "Simon Says No"
  • arena_screen.gd:112, 272"Race friends in a live Simon showdown"
  • game.gd:643 · background_manager.gd"SIMON KING" level-8 banner and its shader comments
  • privacy_policy.html · public/index.html11 occurrences, publicly indexed and trivially findable by counsel. Change these first — they're the cheapest visible proof of good faith.

Deliberately not changing

  • com.raviachstudios.simonImmutable on a live listing. Changing it means publishing a brand-new app and losing 100% of installs, ratings, reviews and ranking signal. Google's own remediation list — title, icons, screenshots, description, developer name — doesn't mention it. It does appear in the store URL, so disclose it in your letter rather than hoping nobody looks.
  • simon-6bc39 (Firebase)Renaming breaks auth, every Firestore path in leaderboard_manager.gd:39,42 and contest_manager.gd:192, and every player's stored data. Not user-visible. Leave it.

Send this early — ideally within 48 hours of Day 0, before the changes are fully live. The point is to start the clock on their side. Fill the yellow fields. Have a real lawyer glance at it if you can (see §10).

I am the developer of the Android application currently listed as "Simon – Memory Challenge" (package com.raviachstudios.simon), published by Raviach Studios. I received Google Play's notice of your client's trademark complaint on [DATE].

I am writing to confirm that I am not disputing your client's rights and am voluntarily remediating. 1. Name. The app is being renamed to "[NEW NAME]". The word "Simon" will not appear in the app title, subtitle, short or long store description, keyword fields, in-app text, promotional material, or my website.

2. Icon and store imagery. The application icon, feature graphic and all screenshots are being replaced. The new artwork does not use a four-quadrant circular layout, a black bezel with a cross divider, or the red/green/blue/yellow primary palette.

3. In-game appearance. The default play control is being changed from a four-quadrant disc to six discrete, separated pads in a distinct palette ([e.g. cyan, violet, magenta, lime, amber, coral]). The heavy black bezel, the cross divider and the central hub plate are being removed. This applies across every visual theme in the app, not only the default one.

4. Descriptive copy. All in-app and store text referring to the device as "Simon" is being rewritten. The app will not describe itself as a version of, or as compatible with, any Hasbro product.

One point I want to raise proactively rather than leave for you to discover. The Android package identifier, com.raviachstudios.simon, was fixed at first publication and cannot be changed on an existing Play listing — changing it would require publishing an entirely new application and would delete the existing user base and their saved progress. It is not displayed anywhere in the app or in the Play Store interface, though it does appear in the listing URL. Google's own remediation guidance in the notice (title, icons, screenshots, description, developer name) does not identify the package identifier as something to change. I would be grateful if your client would accept its retention. If that is not acceptable I would welcome a conversation about a workable alternative, but I want to be transparent that it is not something I can quietly fix.

I am proceeding with all of the above regardless of your response. Once the changes are live I will write again with the updated listing URL and screenshots so you can confirm they meet your client's requirements. If they do, I would ask that you notify Google Play that the complaint is withdrawn, since Google's process places the app at risk of restriction on [DATE — Day 7] and a restriction would prevent me from publishing the very changes you are asking for.

Thank you for the specificity of the notice — identifying both the name and the imagery made it possible to address this properly. If there is anything in the app you consider objectionable that I have not listed above, please tell me and I will address it.

§ 09

What not to say

Written statements in a dispute are difficult to walk back and can surface later as evidence. A few specific traps:

  • Never say "I didn't know it was trademarked." It's not a defence, and it reads as an admission that you were using it. Say what you're doing about it instead.
  • Never argue the mark is invalid, generic, or that "lots of apps use Simon." It's not true here, it antagonises the one person who can withdraw the complaint, and it converts a cheap administrative fix into a legal position you'd have to fund.
  • Never say your game "is" or "is based on" Simon. Not in the letter, not in the store description, not in a review reply. This is the exact thing Hasbro's counsel objected to in the Risk matter — "the classic Risk board game" in the description was a separate complaint from the name.
  • Don't quantify your revenue, downloads, or how long you've been using the name unless asked. It converts a takedown request into a damages conversation.
  • Don't go quiet. Silence for six days then a rushed fix on day seven is the worst possible sequence. Early contact is what made the Risk matter resolve in a week.
  • Don't ask for a licence. Hasbro will not licence SIMON to an indie memory game, and asking positions you as someone who wants to keep using it.
  • Don't half-rebrand. "SimOn", "Symon", "Simone", "S1mon", "Simon's Challenge" — a near-miss rename converts an inadvertent-infringement story into a wilful-infringement story. Wilfulness is what turns a takedown into a lawsuit.
§ 10

The seven days

Day 0 is the date on Google's email — find it before you use this schedule. If more than two days have already passed, compress everything into the front.

  1. Day 0–1
    Find the date. Pick the name. Send the letter.

    Screen two or three candidates against USPTO and Play search, pick one, and get the §07 letter out. This is the highest-leverage hour of the whole week — everything after it runs in parallel while their side reads it.

  2. Day 1
    Fix the public web surfaces.

    public/index.html and privacy_policy.html — 11 occurrences, indexed by search engines, changeable in an hour. Free, visible proof of good faith that costs you nothing and can be pointed to immediately.

  3. Day 1–3
    Redesign the wheel and the icon.

    Six segments by default, new palette, no bezel, no cross, no hub plate — applied at the geometry layer so every skin inherits it. Then the new wordmark for the home screen, and the icon set at 192 / 432 / 512.

  4. Day 2–4
    Sweep the strings and build.

    Work the §06 file list. Change config/name, bump the version, build the AAB, install it on a device and walk every screen — home, how to play, shop, arena, contests, badges, consent dialog, game over. Anything a screenshot could catch.

  5. Day 4
    Reshoot the store art and submit.

    New screenshots from the new build. New feature graphic. Rewrite the description with no "Simon" in any field. Submit the listing update and the AAB together. Review takes time — this is the real deadline, not day 7.

  6. Day 5
    Reply to Google.

    Send §08 through the Console, referencing the submitted update and the date you contacted the complainant.

  7. Day 5–6
    Second letter to counsel — the one that ends it.

    Once the update is live: send the new listing URL, the new icon and two or three screenshots, and ask directly for withdrawal of the complaint. This is the ask that actually closes the matter — Google won't clear it on your say-so.

  8. Day 7+
    If it's still open.

    If you've had no reply, follow up once, attach the evidence again, and copy Google. If the app is restricted anyway, don't panic — restriction is reversible on the complainant's confirmation, and by then you'll have a complete written record showing you did everything the notice asked, early.

§ 11

Afterwards — don't be here again

  • Register the new name. Once you've picked one and it's clean, file it — US Class 9 and 41, and Israel if you want home protection. It's a few hundred dollars and it means the next person who copies you is the one writing this memo.
  • Keep the whole record. Google's notice, both letters, counsel's replies, before/after screenshots, build dates. If this ever resurfaces, a documented week of prompt voluntary compliance is the difference between "resolved" and "wilful."
  • Screen before you name. USPTO search plus a Play Store search, for every future app, before you write a line of code. Ten minutes.
  • Watch the art, not just the words. The trade dress half of this complaint is the half most developers never see coming. If a design deliberately evokes a famous product, that's the risk — regardless of what you call it.
  • The classic palette can come back — as a purchase. A red/green/blue/yellow colour pack that a player chooses to equip is a materially different posture from you shipping it as your brand identity. Not zero risk, but a different and much smaller kind. Revisit it in six months, not now.

Standing

I am not a lawyer and this is not legal advice. It is a researched analysis with primary sources cited so you can check every claim in it. The registration status, patent claims, case holdings and Play policy language above are all verifiable at the links below — check them.

For a Hasbro notice with a hard deadline, an hour with a real IP attorney before the letter goes out is money well spent, and cheap next to the alternative. But the engineering work is unblocked regardless of what they say, and starting it today is right under any advice you'll get.
